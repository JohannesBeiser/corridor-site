The day

Everything on one screen.

The map and the elevation profile sit together and stay in sync. The map is a corridor along the trail — up is ahead, down is behind — and it turns as you scroll so the line always points up. You see the most trail a screen can hold.

Above it, the three numbers you actually want: how far to the next water, the next place to sleep, the next town.

Before you go

Every trail. Free.

No account, no subscription, nothing to unlock. Every trail, every map.

Each one opens on its own page: the season, the two directions' start windows, and what the trail asks of you before you start — registration, permits, the crossings that are boats. Every claim carries a link to the authority it came from.

Alternates

Take any alternate.

Every row shows the two numbers you decide on — what it costs you in distance and in climb. Switch one on and the whole trail re-routes through it: the distance, the climb and the elevation profile all follow.

And you can draw your own. Tap along any track on the map and it is measured correctly, kept, and treated like any other alternate from then on.

Planning

Plan the next sections.

Pick where you are stopping and the section tells you what it costs: the distance, the climb, how many nights, and the longest dry stretch — counting only reliable water, and again counting every source the map has.

Then it says what is worth knowing before you walk in. Permits, river crossings, seasonal closures, long water carries.

The ground

Look at any place in 3D.

Draw a circle anywhere on the map and see that piece of country in three dimensions, with the trail and your own position on it.

Lowest, highest, steepest — for the hillside you are about to be standing on.

What it answers

  • How far to the next water, the next place to sleep, the next town.
  • What's worth knowing before the next section — permits, river crossings, seasonal closures, long water carries.
  • How far you've walked today and what's left to where you're stopping.
  • What an alternate costs you, before you commit to it.

It works with no signal at all

A trail's whole topographic map downloads once and works with the phone in airplane mode. No signal, no server, no account. Contours, hillshading, ground cover, huts, water, towns and the roads you walk in on.

A note on the data

Every waypoint comes from the trail's own association or from open data. I can't hand-verify all of them, and no water source is truly reliable until someone who was there says so. If you're out and something's wrong, tell me and I'll fix it.

Corridor is a planning and navigation aid, not a substitute for a paper map, a compass, or the trail organisation's own current notices.

— Pez, who walked the CDT, the PCT, the GDT and Te Araroa, and built this because he wanted one app that answered the questions he actually had out there.
